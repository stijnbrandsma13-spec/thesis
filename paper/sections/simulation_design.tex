\section{Simulation}
\label{sec:simulation}
To study the conservativeness of the confidence intervals we run a Monte Carlo experiment of $M = 250$ repetitions.
In each repetition we draw a dataset from a known data generating process (DGP) and fit the FKRB estimator once.
Because the DGP is known, we know the true support-point probabilities $\bm{\theta}$ and every functional of them, so we can check directly whether each interval covers the truth.
All DGPs place the random coefficients in two dimensions, which keeps interpretation and visualization clean while still exercising the multidimensional nature of the FKRB estimator.

We compare the three interval constructions of \cref{sec:Inference}: the original FKRB interval \cref{eq:CIFKRB,eq:CIfunctional}, the sieve Wald interval \cref{eq:CIChenPouzo}, and the studentized sieve QLR interval \cref{eq:CIQLR}.
We build all three for every support-point probability $\theta_r$ and for the two coordinate means $\mathbb{E}[\beta_1]$ and $\mathbb{E}[\beta_2]$, forming the mean intervals separately for each coordinate.

We fit every construction under a feasible GLS weighting (\cref{sec:FKRBGLS}) rather than the identity weighting of the original FKRB criterion.
The FKRB regression is heteroskedastic by construction, because the error $\zeta_{ij}$ carries the variance of a binary choice indicator, so reweighting improves efficiency over the identity-weighted criterion \citep{Greene2019}.
The sieve Wald and QLR intervals of \citet{ChenPouzo2015} attain their simplest form under optimal weighting; because the weighting we use is feasible but not optimal (see \cref{sec:implementation}), we rely on the studentized QLR statistic of \cref{eq:QLRnonoptimal}, which remains asymptotically pivotal under non-optimal weighting.
Standard errors throughout come from the individual-clustered, heteroskedasticity-robust sandwich of \cref{eq:FKRBclustervcov}.

We evaluate every interval at two significance levels, $\alpha = 0.05$ and $\alpha = 0.5$.
The former matches the level most common in the literature.
The latter is deliberately large; as \cref{sec:results} shows, coverage at $\alpha = 0.05$ is often close to one, and the higher level gives a more complete picture of how severe the conservativeness is.

The details on the numerical implementation of the simulation can be found in \cref{sec:implementation}.
We highlight a couple of things here.
We use a suboptimal GLS weighting, even though the optimal weighting is known.
The suboptimal weighting matrix is a diagonal matrix, which simplifies the matrix inversion of \cref{eq:FKRBGLSest}.

We use the nonnegative LASSO formulation of the FKRB estimator of \citet{HeissHetzeneckerOsterhaus2022}.
We first try solving it with only the nonnegative constraint \eqref{eq:FKRBNNLconstraint2}, removing the sum-to-one constraint \eqref{eq:FKRBNNLconstraint1}.
This allows us to use the fast active-set algorithm \citep{LawsonHanson1995}.
If this solution does not satisfy the sum-to-one constraint we trace the full path of the coordinate descent algorithm of \citet{FriedmanHastieTibshirani2010}.
We estimate the unique point where constraint \eqref{eq:FKRBNNLconstraint1} is binding by linear interpolation between the two points on the path that cross the constraint.
This strategy is the same as in \citet{Brand2023}.

Especially on the bigger grid $R=225$, some matrices can be severely ill-conditioned.
Therefore, when there is a risk of ill-conditioning, we use the Moore-Penrose pseudoinverse instead of a regular inverse.

To invert the QLR statistic we use Brent's method \citep{Brent1973}.
This part is the most expensive of the simulation.
In each iteration of Brent's algorithm we call the nonnegative LASSO solver, which can already be expensive in and of itself.

\subsection{Data Generating Processes}
\label{sec:dgp}
We generate data according to the random coefficient discrete choice model from \cref{sec:mxl}.
The DGPs are partially inspired by \citet{HeissHetzeneckerOsterhaus2022}.
We adopt their sampling of the exogenous $\bm{x}_{ij}$ uniformly from $[0,5]\times[-3,1]$, i.e. $\bm{x}_{ij} \sim \mathcal{U}([0,5]\times[-3,1])$.
This is the same across all DGPs.
Furthermore, we adopt $N \in \{1\,000, 10\,000\}$.
We thus run our analysis on two sample sizes.
We differ in our choice of $R \in \{25, 225\}$.
In contrast to \citeauthor{HeissHetzeneckerOsterhaus2022}, we only repeat our analysis for two grid sizes, and our large grid size is slightly smaller than theirs because of numerical instability issues.

The main difference with \citeauthor{HeissHetzeneckerOsterhaus2022} is the choice of distributions for the random coefficients.
We consider seven different distributions for $\bm{\beta}_{i}$.
Each of them is a discrete distribution supported on the same regular, equally spaced $\sqrt{R}\times\sqrt{R}$ grid $\tilde{\mathcal{B}}\subset[-4.5, 3.5]^2$.
This is also the same grid that we feed to the FKRB estimator as the prespecified support.
That means $\tilde{\mathcal{B}} = \mathcal{B}$ holds by construction, so any conservativeness we observe is not driven by grid misspecification.

The seven distributions are chosen to vary the prevalence of the boundary problem mentioned in \cref{sec:Inference}.
Whenever the true mass $\theta_r$ at a support point is zero, the parameter sits on the boundary of $\Delta_R$.
By picking distributions ranging from ``no support point on the boundary'' to ``every support point on the boundary'', we obtain a more complete picture of the conservativeness of the FKRB confidence intervals.

\paragraph{Bimodal}
\label{sec:dgp:bimodal}
The first distribution is a discretized mixture of two bivariate normal distributions.
The two components have means $(-2.2, -2.2)$ and $(1.3, 1.3)$ and a common covariance matrix $\scriptsize\begin{pmatrix}
    0.8 & 0.15 \\ 0.15 & 0.8
\end{pmatrix}$, with equal mixture weights.
This places interior mass at two well-separated modes, with near-zero mass in the valley between them.
This distribution is identical to the one used in the second Monte Carlo experiment in \citet{HeissHetzeneckerOsterhaus2022}.
See \cref{fig:dgp:bimodal}.

\paragraph{Tight normal}
\label{sec:dgp:tight}
The second distribution is a discretized bivariate normal distribution with mean $\bm{0}$ and identity covariance matrix.
That is, we set $\theta_r$ proportional to $\phi_{\bm{0},\bm{I}}(\bm{\beta}^{(r)})$ at each grid point $\bm{\beta}^{(r)} \in \tilde{\mathcal{B}}$ and renormalize so that \cref{eq:FKRBconstraint1} holds.
Here, $\phi_{\bm{\mu},\bm{\Sigma}}$ denotes the density of a bivariate normal distribution with mean $\bm{\mu}$ and covariance matrix $\bm{\Sigma}$.
We call this distribution the tight normal because its mass concentrates around the mean, in contrast to the wide normal.
Since the normal density is positive everywhere, all $\theta_r$ are strictly positive.
This means that none of the true parameters lie on the boundary.
We use this distribution as a baseline against which the other distributions can be compared.
See \cref{fig:dgp:tight}.

\paragraph{Wide normal}
\label{sec:dgp:wide}
The third distribution is a discretized bivariate normal distribution with mean $\bm{0}$ and covariance matrix $4\bm{I}$.
The increased variance spreads the mass over the entire grid, which is why we call this distribution the wide normal.
Compared to the tight normal, the renormalized probabilities at each grid point are more uniform and smaller in magnitude.
In contrast to the tight normal, there are more grid points moderately far from the boundary, but fewer grid points far from the boundary.
The structure is the same, however.
This distribution allows us to compare two structurally identical distributions with different boundary problems.
See \cref{fig:dgp:wide}.

\paragraph{Strictly uniform}
\label{sec:dgp:strictuniform}
The fourth distribution puts the exact same mass $\theta_r = 1/R$ on every grid point.
It is the limiting case of the wide normal in \Cref{sec:dgp:wide}, with the mass spread as evenly over the grid as possible.
All $\theta_r$ are strictly positive, so none of the true parameters lie on the boundary, but every parameter approaches the boundary at rate $1/R$ as the grid grows.
The point of this distribution is to study the globally near-boundary setting in its most extreme form, where the true distribution carries no shape information at all.
See \Cref{fig:dgp:strictuniform}.

\paragraph{Almost single type}
\label{sec:dgp:ast}
The fifth distribution puts $90\%$ of the mass on a single grid point near $\bm{0}$.
The remaining $10\%$ is spread uniformly over all other grid points.
Almost every individual thus shares the same coefficient vector, which is why we call this distribution the almost single type.
This creates a single dominant interior parameter and $R-1$ small parameters that lie arbitrarily close to the boundary as $R$ grows.
Note that this distribution still has full support on $\tilde{\mathcal{B}}$, so none of the $\theta_r$ are exactly on the boundary.
The point of this distribution is to push the boundary problem to the extreme while keeping the simplex constraint non-binding.
See \cref{fig:dgp:ast}.

\paragraph{Single type}
\label{sec:dgp:singletype}
The sixth distribution is the degenerate version of the almost single type: it puts all mass on the same grid point near $\bm{0}$ and zero mass on every other grid point.
Every individual now has the same coefficient vector, so no heterogeneity remains and the population consists of a single type.
All $R$ parameters lie exactly on the boundary of $\Delta_R$: $R-1$ of them at $\theta_r = 0$ and the spike itself at $\theta_r = 1$.
The point of this distribution is to study the behavior of the confidence intervals when every single parameter is on the boundary.
See \cref{fig:dgp:singletype}.

\paragraph{Four types}
\label{sec:dgp:fourtypes}
The seventh distribution puts unequal mass on four fixed grid points and zero mass on all remaining $R - 4$ grid points.
The masses are $0.37$, $0.29$, $0.21$, and $0.13$, placed at the grid points closest to $(-3.2,\, 1.6)$, $(-1.0,\, -2.9)$, $(0.6,\, 2.3)$, and $(2.4,\, -0.7)$, respectively.
We drew these four locations and masses at random once and hardcoded them, so they are identical across all repetitions; snapping each location to its nearest grid point keeps the active points on $\tilde{\mathcal{B}}$ for both grid sizes.
Each individual is thus one of four latent types, which is why we call this distribution the four types, matching the latent class setting with a small number of types.
Like the single type, this distribution has structural zeros: $R - 4$ of the true parameters $\theta_r$ are exactly on the boundary.
The point of this distribution is to study the behavior at a realistic latent class distribution.
See \Cref{fig:dgp:fourtypes}.

\subsection{Analysis}
\label{sec:analysis}
We judge each interval construction by its empirical coverage, estimated across the $M = 250$ Monte Carlo repetitions.
For a target parameter $\psi$ and repetition $m$, the hit indicator $H^{(m)}(\psi) = \mathds{1}\{\psi \in \mathrm{CI}^{(m)}_{1-\alpha,\psi}\}$ records whether the interval from that repetition contains the truth.
The Monte Carlo coverage estimate is the hit frequency
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:mc_coverage}
    \hat{c}_{1-\alpha}(\psi) = \frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M} H^{(m)}(\psi),
\end{equation}
a consistent estimate of the true coverage $c_{\mathrm{CI}_{1-\alpha,\psi}}(\psi)$ from \cref{sec:confidenceintervals}.
Under exact coverage \cref{eq:mc_coverage} equals $1-\alpha$ in expectation; values above $1-\alpha$ signal conservativeness and values below it signal under-coverage.

We compute \cref{eq:mc_coverage} for three target parameters and the three interval constructions of \cref{sec:Inference}: the original FKRB interval in \cref{eq:CIFKRB,eq:CIfunctional}, the sieve Wald interval in \cref{eq:CIChenPouzo}, and the studentized sieve QLR interval in \cref{eq:CIQLR}.
For the two means each repetition yields one interval, so the estimate is a single hit frequency per cell.
For $\theta_r$ each repetition yields $R$ intervals, one per grid point, and we report the coverage averaged over both repetitions and grid points,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:mc_coverage_theta}
    \bar{c}_{1-\alpha} = \frac{1}{MR}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\sum_{r=1}^{R} H^{(m)}(\theta_r).
\end{equation}
The grid-level maps that resolve \cref{eq:mc_coverage_theta} point by point appear for every DGP in the figure appendix.

Because $M$ is finite, each reported coverage is itself an estimate, so we attach a $95\%$ confidence interval to it.
The $95\%$ level is fixed and is not the nominal level $1-\alpha$ of the interval under study.
The interval separates genuine conservativeness from Monte Carlo noise: an estimated coverage of $0.97$ counts as over-covering at $\alpha = 0.05$ only when its confidence interval lies above $0.95$.

We bootstrap this interval for $\theta_r$.
The indicators $H^{(m)}(\theta_r)$ are dependent across grid points within a repetition, since all $R$ intervals come from the same dataset and the same fit, so the $MR$ summands in \cref{eq:mc_coverage_theta} are not independent and a binomial interval would understate the variance of $\bar{c}$.
The repetitions are mutually independent, however, because each draws a fresh dataset.
We therefore resample whole repetitions with replacement, treat the repetition as the independent sampling unit, recompute $\bar{c}$ on each of $B = 9{,}999$ resamples, and read off the $2.5$th and $97.5$th percentiles of the resampled coverages \citep{Efron1979,CameronMiller2015}.
Resampling at the repetition level preserves the within-repetition dependence across grid points, and the percentile construction respects the $[0,1]$ range of a coverage even when the hit frequency sits close to one, where a symmetric normal interval would not.

For the two means we do not have to resort to bootstrapping methods.
We use the analytical Wilson score interval \citep{Wilson1927}.
Each repetition now contributes a single Bernoulli hit, so the estimate in \cref{eq:mc_coverage} is a genuine binomial proportion from $M$ independent draws, for which a binomial interval is appropriate.
Writing $\hat{c}$ for the hit frequency \cref{eq:mc_coverage} and $z = \Phi^{-1}(0.975)$, the Wilson interval centers on the shrunken proportion $\tilde{c}$ and extends by the half-width $w$,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:wilson_parts}
    \tilde{c} = \frac{\hat{c} + z^{2}/(2M)}{1 + z^{2}/M},
    \qquad
    w = \frac{z}{1 + z^{2}/M}\sqrt{\frac{\hat{c}(1-\hat{c})}{M} + \frac{z^{2}}{4M^{2}}},
\end{equation}
so that
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:wilson}
    \mathrm{CI}^{\mathrm{Wilson}}_{0.95,\,c} = \left[\tilde{c} - w,\; \tilde{c} + w\right].
\end{equation}

We prefer Wilson over the textbook Wald interval
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:wald}
    \mathrm{CI}^{\mathrm{Wald}}_{0.95,\,c} = \left[\hat{c} - z\sqrt{\frac{\hat{c}(1-\hat{c})}{M}},\; \hat{c} + z\sqrt{\frac{\hat{c}(1-\hat{c})}{M}}\right],
\end{equation}
because the mean-coverage estimates are routinely close to one, exactly where the Wald interval is least reliable.
The Wald interval can extend above one, and its actual coverage degrades for proportions near the boundary \citep{BrownCaiDasGupta2001}.
The Wilson interval instead stays inside $[0,1]$ and keeps actual coverage close to nominal even for small $M$ and extreme proportions \citep{BrownCaiDasGupta2001}.
It also avoids the systematic over-coverage of the exact Clopper-Pearson interval \citep{ClopperPearson1934} noted in \cref{sec:confidenceintervals}, a desirable property when conservativeness is the object of study.
These reasons are also among those given by \citet{Newcombe1998}.
